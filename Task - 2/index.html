01 / Email awareness

How to identify a suspicious email

Phishing messages try to make you act before you think. Check the sender, the message, and the destination before clicking or replying.

✉

Example: a suspicious delivery message

From: Delivery Support <helpdesk@delivery-update.example>

Subject: Urgent — parcel on hold

Your parcel cannot be delivered. Pay a small re-delivery fee within 2 hours or your parcel will be returned.

UrgencyUnexpected paymentUnfamiliar sender

Review delivery details

Training example: This is a fictional message using the reserved .example domain. Do not visit a link or provide payment details from an unexpected message.
⚑

Common warning signs

  • Sender address does not match the organization.
  • Unexpected urgency, threats, or too-good-to-be-true rewards.
  • Requests for passwords, one-time codes, or payment details.
  • Unexpected attachments or links.
  • Generic greetings, unusual wording, or spelling mistakes.
02 / Website awareness

Fake login pages and fraudulent websites

A page can look professional and still be fake. Check the complete domain name and reach important services through a trusted bookmark or by typing their known address yourself.

Inspect the domain

Look for misspellings, extra words, unusual subdomains, or a domain that does not belong to the claimed organization.

HTTPS is not proof

A padlock indicates an encrypted connection. It does not prove that the website itself is genuine or trustworthy.

Avoid login links in messages

Open the official app or type the known website address yourself instead of using an unexpected email or SMS link.

03 / Human manipulation

Common social engineering techniques

Attackers often manipulate emotions and trust rather than exploiting software.

⏱

Urgency and fear

“Your account will be closed in 10 minutes.” The attacker wants you to skip verification.

♙

Impersonation

Someone pretends to be a manager, bank employee, support agent, or trusted contact.

🎁

Reward or curiosity

A prize, refund, job offer, or surprising attachment is used to trigger a quick click.

🔐

Credential harvesting

A fake sign-in page asks for passwords, one-time codes, or account recovery details.

🤝

Trust building

A scammer may use familiar names, copied logos, or information gathered from public profiles.

📞

Pressure by phone

A caller claims to be support and asks you to install remote-access software or share a code.

04 / Practice scenarios

Case studies and real-life style examples

These fictional scenarios are designed for practice. They do not represent a real company or incident.

CASE 01

Parcel re-delivery fee

A message says a parcel is on hold and demands a small fee through an unfamiliar link within two hours.

Red flags: unexpected fee, urgent deadline, unknown sender, and a link that does not match a verified delivery service.

Safer action: check the order in the official delivery app or website. Do not use the message link.

CASE 02

Unexpected recruitment task

A recruiter sends an attachment and asks the candidate to sign in with an email password to view an interview schedule.

Red flags: unexpected sign-in request, pressure to act quickly, and a request unrelated to normal recruitment.

Safer action: verify the recruiter through an independently found company contact and never share an email password or one-time code.

05 / Practical protection

What to do before you click

Use this quick routine whenever a message asks you to sign in, pay, download, or share information.

Pause

Do not react to threats, prizes, or urgent deadlines.

Inspect

Check the sender, full link destination, spelling, and unexpected requests.

Verify

Contact the organization using a trusted app, bookmark, or known phone number.

Report

Use your organization's reporting channel and delete or block the message.

Good security habits

Use MFAUnique passwordsPassword managerUpdate devicesReport suspicious messages

  • Use a password manager and a unique password for every important account.
  • Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA), preferably using a passkey or authenticator app where available.
  • Never share one-time passcodes, recovery codes, or passwords with a caller or message sender.
  • Keep your browser, operating system, and security software updated.
  • If you already clicked or entered details, change the affected password through the official site, revoke suspicious sessions where possible, contact the service provider, and report the incident promptly.
